Look in a mirror. Focus on your left eye. Now switch to your right eye. Back and forth.

Your eyes seem to jump instantly. You never catch them moving.

There they are. Your eyes were moving the whole time.

So why did your mirror seem to hide it?

First, your eyes don't scan the world like a smooth camera. They make rapid jumps between points of interest. Scientists call these jumps saccades.

And they happen constantly. Even as you watch this video, your eyes hop from one detail to another.

Here's the problem.

Every time your eyes jump, the image on your retinas sweeps across at high speed. If you experienced all that motion normally, the world could look like a shaky, smeared video.

Your visual system has a clever response. Around these fast eye movements, your sensitivity to some visual information drops sharply.

It's called saccadic suppression.

But you're not completely blind. Your brain also processes and stitches together information before and after each jump, so your experience of the world remains surprisingly stable.

That's why your friend's eyes visibly dart from side to side, while your own quick movements seem to vanish in the mirror.

And your phone? Its camera doesn't have your brain's visual filtering. It simply records the movement.

Here's the strangest part: this isn't happening only in front of mirrors.

It's happening while you read, watch videos, or look around a room.

Your eyes are constantly moving.

But what you experience isn't a raw recording of everything they capture.

It's the remarkably smooth version your brain helps construct.

The mirror wasn't hiding anything. Your own visual system was.
